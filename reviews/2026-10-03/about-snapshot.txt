  - heading "From Handwritten Notebooks to a Predictive Flood Model" [level=1]
  - paragraph: How 204 storm observations recorded with measuring tapes across 5 years in Mathews County, Virginia uncovered the mathematical tipping points of coastal compound flooding.
  - generic: 
  - heading "Why Regional Weather Reports Fail Mathews County" [level=2]
  - paragraph: Mathews County is an almost sea-level peninsula surrounded by the Chesapeake Bay, Mobjack Bay, and the Piankatank River. Much of the land sits less than 5 to 10 feet above sea level.
  - paragraph: When TV forecasts in Richmond or Norfolk announce a "Coastal Flood Warning," it offers almost zero practical value to a local homeowner. It doesn’t tell you whether the water will stay in the ditch or flood the driveway, what time high tide will block your car, or whether an SUV can get through.
  - strong: "The Core Question:"
  - text: "\"At exactly what river stage does the water breach our ditches, and how many inches of water does every additional tenth of a foot create?\""
  - heading "Community observation archive" [level=2]
  - paragraph: Historical community observations inform the model. Original notebooks and individual observer records are retained privately. The Data page provides yearly numerical summaries, while the Science page explains aggregate benchmarks and their limitations.

  - heading " The Three Physical Breakthroughs" [level=2]:
    - generic: 
    - text: The Three Physical Breakthroughs
  - generic: 3.99 ft
  - heading "1. The Tipping Point" [level=3]
  - paragraph: Below 3.99 ft MLLW, water remains in ditches (0" flooding). Above 4.00 ft, ditch banks breach and water spreads across the driveway.
  - generic: 10.95" / ft
  - heading "2. The Inundation Slope" [level=3]
  - paragraph: Every 0.10 ft of river rise yields 1.1 to 1.2 inches of water depth on the property ($r = 0.912$, $R^2 = 0.832$).
  - generic: β Restriction
  - heading "3. Compound Pluvial Physics" [level=3]
  - paragraph: Heavy rainfall cannot drain by gravity when high bay water acts as a "cork in the ditch," trapping rain directly onto driveways.
